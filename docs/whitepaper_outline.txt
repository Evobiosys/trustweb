TITLE
======================================================================
TrustInTheWeb: asking the people you trust, one hop further
Working subtitle: A first-person protocol for finding people and things through people you already trust
(Product name in prose: TrustWeb. Site name where the site names itself: TrustInTheWeb.org. Written by Jakob Possert-Bienzle, EvoBioSys, Vienna.)

======================================================================
ABSTRACT (one paragraph, about 120 words)
======================================================================
When we look for funding, a flat, a doctor or an introduction, we would rather ask people we trust than a platform. Acquaintances are a large source of new
opportunities, but a person can only connect people they personally know, and reposting into a group chat reaches few. TrustWeb is an infrastructure that lets a
question travel through the rings of people you trust, one consent at a time, so the answer comes back with the path it took. Each person keeps their notes and
their inventory on their own device, chooses per line who may ask, and a decline looks exactly like "nothing found". Today a question reaches a person's direct
contacts; the next hop is designed and shown in a scripted demo. This paper states the problem, the philosophy behind it, the mechanism, what exists, and the plan.

1. THE PROBLEM  (lead with the problem, then the philosophy)

1.1 Finding people and things through people you trust
  - We look for a job, a grant, a flat, a cleaner, a doctor, a loan of a cargo bike; we would rather ask friends for leads than strangers. [C: own site copy, idea2.site/trustweb, 2026-10-07]
  - Acquaintances are a major source of new opportunities, but recalling "who would fit" is memory work with little follow-through, because nobody is paid to do it. [A: Rajkumar et al., Science, 2022-09-15 (job mobility on LinkedIn, weak ties help, with an inverted-U shape and industry differences); the "no one is paid to do it" half is Jakob's own argument [C]]
  - A superconnector can only connect people they know. Reposting is a poor channel. [A: Goel, Anderson, Hofman, Watts, Management Science 62(1), 2016: only about 1 in 3,000 shared items grew into a large cascade.] The live site says "under 4% of matches": [source needed] (Goel et al. does not state that figure; find its origin or drop it).
  - Net effect: poor throughput of real information between people who would gladly help each other.
1.2 Where a platform is the wrong tool (strongest first, from the site's "specifically useful" list, [C])
  - Only locally relevant (leftover food tonight); trust is the main issue (house-sitting, care work); not publicly possible (subletting without the landlord knowing); only certain rings should see it;
    below the bar for a public listing (a bike, a spare room); honesty about flaws (a used car); fair pricing between people who trust each other.
1.3 Trust versus platforms
  - Platforms broadcast and rank; trust networks route and consent. A no on a platform is visible; in TrustWeb a decline is indistinguishable from no match, so refusing stays socially free. [B: repo DECISIONS.md D22, D27, D28; README Privacy Honesty Box]
  - Willingness to be asked is the scarce resource; every design rule protects it. (Jakob, signal-orbit nucleus 2026-09-14 [C]; see 2.2)
1.4 "We lead with the querying, not the recording" (Jakob 10-07 11:44 [C]): recording and notes are how the inventory fills; querying is what is new.
  [Anomaly for the site agent, not for this paper: the live "How it works" section says "Taking notes comes first". Jakob's 10-07 rule is the reverse. Not edited here.]

Sources and what each means for TrustWeb: section 2A.7. [locators to confirm]

Frame: his terms, "first person protocol", with one core and several formats (Signal Orbit: a core text plus orbits for each audience; this paper is the mini white paper drawn from the core and from the applications; Jakob 10-07 08:47 [C]).
2.1 Trust we already carry
  - His words: "The social contract and trust contract our hearts already carry": the system scales it, lets it work without our brains, and gives each other access beyond what we can convey in words quickly. [C: signal-orbit nucleus v0.3, 2026-09-14]
  - Site line: "We have always trusted our friends, and now we have a way to represent it digitally." [C: idea2.site/trustweb, 2026-10-07]. The word "always" links to 2.5.
  - First person: each person's device holds their own record; "the owner's device is the truth"; no server decides. [C: nucleus essential 8; B: repo docs/30-architecture-decisions.md]
2.2 What this protects
  - "Trust grows with use; what depletes is willingness to be asked. Every design rule protects that willingness." [C: nucleus]
  - "Reach cannot be generated, only met." Bots cannot join, because joining costs a meeting. [C: nucleus, ruling 2026-09-14]
  - The question travels, the data stays: consent chain, not transitive trust computation; a friend of a friend is not automatically a friend. [C: nucleus essential 4]
  - Paths and provenance, no scalar trust score, ever; three rings (close friend, friend, acquaintance), never a continuous number. [C: nucleus essential 6; site "discreet and discrete"]
2.3 Trust is not static, and not symmetric  (from Jakob's own TrustWeb "concepts" notes, Tana w7fWTRC7ZWds, 2026 [C])
  - Concepts he lists: hops; strength of connection; "trustworthiness is not static" ("if it's a friend I also want to show up more trustworthily").
  - "Three [strength of connection] links/[hops] to a person can be stronger than two weak ones." Candidate design principle for later multi-hop weighting. [to confirm: his notes, no source beyond himself]
  - Zutrauen and Vertrauen are listed as a concept pair (trust in someone's ability versus trust in their good faith) but the Tana nodes hold no text, and the signal-orbit file marks the moral-versus-competence distinction INTERNAL ONLY. ❗ Jakob decides whether it enters a public paper.   - "In terms of what people query, they query their friends, not other people." (Jakob, Tana BI_4kdn-iTit, 2026-09-24 [C]) : the first product bet. Hypothesis, not finding: [source needed] (no user data yet).

2.5 Our closest relatives (anchor #bonobos on the website: whitepaper.html#bonobos)
  - What the sources say, plainly (text for the website in the last part of this file):
    - Chimpanzees trust bonded friends more than non-friends in a non-verbal trust game: Engelmann and Herrmann, Current Biology, 2016 [A]. 15 chimpanzees, one sanctuary.
    - Wild baboon females with stronger, more stable social bonds live longer: Silk et al., Current Biology, 2010 [A]; bonds raise infant survival: Silk, Alberts, Altmann, Science, 2003 [A].
    - Bonobos: sanctuary-living bonobos shared food with strangers and, in one test, nine of fourteen chose the stranger first: Tan and Hare, PLoS ONE, 2013 [A for the paper; the "nine of fourteen" figure came from a university press summary, Duke Today, 2013-01 [B], verify in the paper]. Follow-up: young bonobos helped unfamiliar non-group members get food without being asked, plus contagious yawning toward stranger videos: Tan, Ariely, Hare, Scientific Reports 7, 2017 [A].
    - Bonobo cooperation when food is monopolisable: Hare et al., Current Biology, 2007 [A]; a 2023 conceptual replication (Nolte, Sterck, van Leeuwen, Royal Society Open Science) found the cooperation result replicated but the tolerance (co-feeding) result did not [A, read from a search summary only; full text not fetched].
    - Wider mammals: friendships lasting years in horses, elephants, hyenas, dolphins, monkeys, chimpanzees: Seyfarth and Cheney, Annual Review of Psychology, 2012 [A]; vampire bats: Carter and Wilkinson, Proc. R. Soc. B, 2013 [A]. Described as convergent evolution, not one inherited trait.
  - How it grounds "we have always trusted our friends": the chimpanzee and baboon results show selective, durable trust toward bonded partners in our nearest relatives (confidence 0.9 for "older than our species"; Jakob's earlier research note puts it at 0.95). The bonobo results add that bonds can also reach outward to unfamiliar individuals, which supports the weak-tie, one-hop-further thesis.
  - Honest limits: small samples, mostly sanctuary-living juveniles, no trust measure in the bonobo studies, no claim about wild populations or about humans; the human claim is an inference by analogy, not a result. Do not write "bonobos trust their friends": the direct trust-game evidence is chimpanzee.
  - ❗ Jakob's lines ("the 'always' ... bonobos") name bonobos, but his own earlier research (research_friendship-deep-time_2026-09-26, Tana j7YaUjve2RVb) rests on chimpanzees, baboons and bats. See report. The section is written to cover both.

One-sentence thesis for the paper: five decades of network research say that new information arrives through acquaintances and bridges, that short paths exist but people rarely pass a request along, and that earlier systems either measured the network, searched it from a central server, or authenticated keys; TrustWeb makes the passing-along cheap and consent-based, and keeps the data on each person's device.

2A.2 Weak ties carry new information  (the core of the section)
  Granovetter 1973 and 1983
  - Result: tie strength is a mix of time, emotional intensity, intimacy and reciprocal services. People close to us tend to know the same people, so what they tell us is redundant; acquaintances sit in other clusters, and weak ties are the usual bridges between clusters, so new information reaches us through them. (confidence 0.9; Granovetter 1973, abstract read in wave 3) The 1974 book Getting a Job (Newton, Massachusetts, professional and managerial workers) found that most people who got their job through a contact saw that contact only occasionally or rarely; the figures 16.7, 55.6 and 27.8 percent for "often, occasionally, rarely" are from memory. (recall; confidence 0.75, locator to confirm in the book)
  - The 1983 revisit keeps the thesis and adds the counterweight: weak ties give access to information and resources outside our circle, but strong ties are more motivated to help and are easier to reach. (confidence 0.85; recall) Late citations of "weak ties" often drop this second half.
  - Means for TrustWeb: asking only close friends mostly returns what you already know; the acquaintance ring and the second hop are where new leads come from. And because strong ties carry the motivation, TrustWeb routes the second hop through a consenting strong tie: a trusted introducer who vouches, which combines both halves.
  - Claim for the paper: "Acquaintances are a major source of new opportunities" (already in the abstract); "the people best placed to help are often not the ones closest to you".
  - Evidence needed: Granovetter 1973, 1983 [A]; a causal modern test (Rajkumar et al., 2A.5); for TrustWeb itself, nothing yet: pilot measure = share of useful answers that came from the acquaintance ring or from hop two versus the close ring. Hypothesis until then.
  Marsden and Campbell 1984, Krackhardt 1992 (recall)
  - Result: self-reported closeness is a better measure of tie strength than contact frequency or duration (Marsden and Campbell); strong ties ("philos") matter most under uncertainty and change because they supply trust (Krackhardt). (confidence 0.8 each; locators to confirm)
  - Means for TrustWeb: the three self-chosen rings (close friend, friend, acquaintance) are a defensible way to measure tie strength; they match the better of the two measures. A ring is not a score (2.2).
  - Claim: rings are a coarse, person-chosen version of tie strength. Evidence needed: Marsden and Campbell [A]; Jakob's confirmation that rings are meant this way (open question 14).
  Granovetter 1985, embeddedness (recall)
  - Result: economic action (hiring, lending, trading) is embedded in networks of personal relations, and trust in transactions comes mostly from those relations, not from rules or markets alone. (confidence 0.85; AJS 91(3), 481 to 510, locator to confirm)
  - Means for TrustWeb: a question answered through people you know is how trust already operates in exchange; TrustWeb carries the path along. Evidence: Granovetter 1985 [A].

2A.3 Bridges, brokers and social capital
  Burt 1992 (book), 2004 (article)
  - Result: gaps between clusters are "structural holes"; people who span them see varied information, get ideas valued more, and gain more from brokering. (confidence 0.8; Burt 2004 AJS 110(2), 349 to 399, register, DOI metadata confirmed, findings from search summary)
  - Means for TrustWeb: every person with contacts in two circles is a potential bridge; an ask that crosses their circles is exactly the case TrustWeb makes cheap. Tension to name: a broker's advantage comes from being the only link, and an introduction protocol makes that link easier to copy. Hypothesis, no evidence yet: introductions through TrustWeb raise, not lower, the broker's standing because the path shows who made the connection.
  - Claim: "TrustWeb makes bridging cheap, and shows who bridged." Evidence needed: Burt [A]; pilot: number of introductions that completed, and whether introducers keep or lose standing (ask them).
  Lin, Ensel and Vaughn 1981; Lin 2001; Coleman 1988; Putnam 2000
  - Result (Lin): social capital is the resources embedded in a network and reached through ties; weak ties help most when they reach contacts of higher status ("strength of position"). (confidence 0.75; recall; Lin, Ensel, Vaughn, American Sociological Review 46(4), 393 to 405, locator to confirm)
  - Result (Coleman): networks with closure (people who all know each other) make trust and sanctions work; this is the other side of the Granovetter and Burt argument, strong closure for trust, bridges for novelty. (confidence 0.8; AJS 94 supplement, S95 to S120, locator to confirm)
  - Result (Putnam): bonding social capital (inward, exclusive) supports mutual support among similar people; bridging social capital (outward, inclusive) links across groups; the book argues American civic life shrank in the late twentieth century. (confidence 0.85; chapter 1, page to confirm) A European or Austrian counterpart is missing here (open question 12).
  - Means for TrustWeb: TrustWeb is a bridging tool built on bonding trust: first hop = bonding, second hop = bridging, with the introducer's consent as the trust link.
  - Claim: "trust holds within groups; novelty arrives across them; TrustWeb connects the two on request". Evidence needed: Lin, Coleman, Putnam [A]; a Europe or Austria source for the wording "in Austria, informal contacts find jobs and flats" (not searched yet).

2A.4 Small worlds, six steps, and why requests die  (the section that justifies "one hop further" and "willingness is the scarce resource")
  Pool and Kochen (written about 1958, published 1978); Milgram 1967; Travers and Milgram 1969
  - Result: Milgram asked starters in Nebraska and Boston to forward a letter, through personal acquaintances only, to a target in the Boston area. In Travers and Milgram 1969, 64 of 296 chains (about 22 percent) arrived, with a mean of about 5.2 intermediaries. (confidence 0.8; Sociometry 32(4), 425 to 443; counts from memory, locator to confirm.) Most chains stopped because someone did not pass the letter on. The words "six degrees of separation" belong to later popular culture (the 1990 play of that name; confidence 0.85), not to Milgram's papers.
  - Means for TrustWeb: short paths between strangers exist, but the weak point is a person not passing the request on. That is the design problem TrustWeb is built around: make forwarding and declining cost nothing socially (the indistinguishable no, 3.4) and cost little effort (one tap).
  - Claim: "In the best-known small-world experiments the limit was willingness to pass a request on, not the absence of a path." Evidence needed: Travers and Milgram [A]; Dodds et al. below; for TrustWeb, pilot measure: answer rate and time to answer per ask per hop.
  - Caution: Milgram's completion rates and samples are contested (Kleinfeld 2002, Society 39(2), 61 to 66; recall; confidence 0.7; locator to confirm). Do not write "six degrees" as a fact about TrustWeb's reach.
  Watts and Strogatz 1998 (Nature 393, 440 to 442; recall; confidence 0.9; pages from memory, locator to confirm)
  - Result: take a tidy ring-shaped network with high clustering and rewire a few links at random; average path length collapses while clustering stays high. Examples in the paper: the neural network of the worm C. elegans, the western US power grid, film-actor collaboration. A few weak, long-range ties make a world small.
  - Means for TrustWeb: weak ties are the shortcuts; this is why a second hop can matter far more than the first, and why TrustWeb need not plan for a third.
  - Claim: "a few bridging ties shorten the distance between any two people a great deal". Evidence needed: Watts and Strogatz [A]. NOT claimable for TrustWeb's own early network (hundreds of people): measure, do not extrapolate.
  Kleinberg 2000 (Nature 406, 845; confidence 0.85, locator to confirm)
  - Result: short paths existing is one thing, finding them using only local knowledge another; decentralised search works when long ties follow a particular distance pattern.
  - Means for TrustWeb: nobody holds the whole graph here either (local-first); the protocol asks each ring in turn and lets each person decide, rather than computing a route. Evidence: Kleinberg [A]; design argument, not a result for TrustWeb.
  Dodds, Muhamad and Watts 2003 (Science 301(5634), 827 to 829; confidence 0.85 for the reference)
  - Result: a global email version of Milgram's test: tens of thousands of chains toward 18 targets in 13 countries; only a small fraction finished (about 384 of 24,163, from memory, confidence 0.7, locator to confirm). The authors attribute the failures mainly to people not forwarding, not to missing short paths. (confidence 0.75)
  - Means and evidence as for Milgram: the willingness bottleneck replicates at scale.

2A.5 Weak ties, measured at scale
  - Onnela et al. 2007 (PNAS 104(18), 7332 to 7336; recall; confidence 0.85): in a mobile phone network, weak ties hold the large-scale structure together; removing them breaks the network apart faster than removing strong ties. Means: weak ties are structural, not decoration. Evidence: Onnela [A].
  - Bakshy, Rosenn, Marlow, Adamic 2012 (WWW 2012, pp. 519 to 528; recall; confidence 0.85): on Facebook, a person is more likely to share what close friends shared, but weak ties as a group supply more of the novel information because there are so many of them. Means: the information value of acquaintances is large even when each is less active. Evidence: Bakshy [A].
  - Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson, Aral 2022 (Science 377(6612), 15 Sept 2022; register; DOI 10.1126/science.abl4476, metadata confirmed; pages to confirm): randomised experiments on about 20 million LinkedIn users between 2015 and 2019. Moderately weak ties were the most useful for job mobility (an inverted U), and the best strength differed by industry: weaker ties helped more in digital industries, stronger ties in less digital ones. (confidence 0.8; from search summaries) This is the strongest modern evidence and not older than 10 years.
  - Means for TrustWeb: the ring design should not assume "weaker is always better"; the acquaintance ring is the new-information ring, the friend ring the best-balance ring, and TrustWeb lets each person choose. Caution: all three studies measure information and job mobility, not favours or borrowing a cargo bike; the step to TrustWeb's use cases is an inference (confidence 0.6, ⚠️).
  - Willingness, the other half (register): people underestimate how willing others are to help (Zhao and Epley, Psychological Science 33(10), 2022) and overestimate the cost of declining an invitation (Givi and Kirk, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 2024); questions to Facebook friends get useful answers but carry social costs (Gray et al., ACM CSCW 2013; Ellison et al., AAAI ICWSM 2013). Means: asking is worth lowering the cost of; supports 1.3 and 3.4. Evidence: the four papers [A]; findings from search summaries or abstracts only, confidence 0.65 to 0.75 per the wave 3 list ⚠️ (the lower end is below 0.7).

2A.6 Asking your network: search, introductions, and why they died
  Aardvark (Horowitz and Kamvar, "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Social Search Engine", WWW 2010, pp. 431 to 440; register; numbers from a TechCrunch summary only)
  - Result: a service that took a question and routed it by instant message or email to friends and friends of friends judged likely to know the answer; answerers could decline by typing "pass" and received under about one request a day (Wikipedia, confidence 0.8). Launched about 2007, bought by Google in 2010 (confidence 0.85, recall), closed in 2011 (VentureBeat 2011).
  - Means for TrustWeb: the nearest historical ancestor of "ask your web". Same shape (a question travels through acquaintances to people who may decline), different locus: Aardvark's servers held the profiles and chose the answerers; in TrustWeb the asker's rings choose, the inventory stays on each phone, and a decline is invisible. Lessons: low-volume, easy-decline asks worked; a standalone product needed a reason beyond question and answer.
  - Claim: "this has been tried, centrally, and was liked; it closed inside a larger company." Evidence needed: the 2010 paper and the TechCrunch and VentureBeat items [A/B]; do NOT quote response-time or completion numbers until read in the paper (the W3 collectors could not fetch it; HTTP 404 on one URL, PDF locator in the register).
  - Neighbours (register, tier C and B): SixDegrees.com 1997 to 2000 (an early "contacts of contacts" network), Jelly 2014 to 2017, LinkedIn Introductions; all central services, none local-first.
  Honest scope: of the 42 systems the wave 3 agent checked, none combined multi-hop asking, per-hop consent and local-first storage; absence of evidence, confidence 0.6 ⚠️. Re-run the search before NLnet.

2A.7 Trust networks and web-of-trust systems since 1991
  PGP and OpenPGP
  - Result: Phil Zimmermann released PGP in 1991; its web of trust, in which users sign each other's keys and mark others as trusted introducers, is described in the PGP 2.0 era documentation of 1992 (confidence 0.8; "The Official PGP User's Guide", MIT Press 1995, locator to confirm). GnuPG's default rule needs one fully trusted or three marginally trusted signatures over a path of limited depth (confidence 0.8; GnuPG manual, locator to confirm). Standardised as OpenPGP (IETF RFC 4880, 2007; replaced by RFC 9580, 2024; confidence 0.85, locators to confirm).
  - Usability evidence: Whitten and Tygar, "Why Johnny Can't Encrypt", USENIX Security 1999, pp. 169 to 184 (recall; confidence 0.85): test users mostly failed to use PGP correctly.
  - Means: trust levels (none, marginal, full) are an old idea and a ring in disguise; the failure was usability and key management, which argues for TrustWeb's coarse, person-chosen rings. But PGP answers "is this key really theirs", not "who can help me".
  Advogato (Raph Levien, about 1999; Wikipedia register; shut down 2016, Wikipedia)
  - Result: members certified each other as Apprentice, Journeyer or Master; an attack-resistant trust metric computed who counted, seeded by trusted accounts (confidence 0.75; recall; locator to confirm Levien's text, for example the chapter in Golbeck, ed., Computing with Social Trust, Springer 2009).
  - Means: a global trust metric needs a trusted seed and one computed answer; TrustWeb has no global score on purpose (2.2). Advogato is dead; its idea lives on elsewhere.
  EigenTrust and trust propagation (recall)
  - Kamvar, Schlosser, Garcia-Molina, "The EigenTrust Algorithm for Reputation Management in P2P Networks", WWW 2003, pp. 640 to 651 (confidence 0.9): global reputation computed by repeating local trust values until they converge. The first author, Sepandar Kamvar, is also the second author of the Aardvark paper (confidence 0.9).
  - Guha, Kumar, Raghavan, Tomkins, "Propagation of Trust and Distrust", WWW 2004, pp. 403 to 412 (confidence 0.85): trust and distrust can be propagated across a graph; propagation of distrust is the harder part.
  - Jøsang, Ismail, Boyd, "A survey of trust and reputation systems for online service provision", Decision Support Systems 43(2), 2007, 618 to 644 (confidence 0.85): the standard overview.
  - Means: the computing line turns trust into a number and a path calculation; TrustWeb's choice (paths and provenance, no scalar score, consent per hop) departs from it deliberately. Claim: "a design choice, justified by usability and by the cost of being wrong about a person, not by a measured result" (⚠️ argument, confidence 0.6).
  Keybase (Wikipedia and CNBC 2020 in the register; BSD-3-Clause licence per the register)
  - Result: launched about 2014 (recall, confidence 0.8); proved identity by linking social accounts and devices; acquired by Zoom in May 2020 (CNBC, 2020-05-07), development unclear since. Means: identity proofs, not asking; and a reminder that a company can retire a trust network. Evidence: CNBC [B], Wikipedia [C], repo licence [B].
  The present field: DIDs, DIDComm, KERI, OpenVTC, Rebooting the Web of Trust
  - W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) v1.0 became a W3C Recommendation in July 2022 (confidence 0.9; locator to confirm). KERI, Samuel Smith, "Key Event Receipt Infrastructure (KERI)", arXiv 2019 (confidence 0.75, identifier to confirm). The Rebooting the Web of Trust workshops began about 2015 (confidence 0.75, locator to confirm). OpenVTC (the First Person Project's trust-community tools, Apache-2.0 per the register) is the nearest living project; the register says no multi-hop asking.

  - Claim: "identity and key trust were solved piece by piece; the question of who can help me through people I trust has had fewer attempts and no living, local-first one." Evidence: this subsection, plus the W3 prior-art list; confidence 0.6 ⚠️ until the search is re-run.

2A.8 What this means for TrustWeb: the claims this section lets the paper make, and the evidence each still needs
  C1 Querying your connections works as human behaviour. Support: asking friends is common and useful, with social costs (Gray 2013; Ellison 2013; Zhao and Epley 2022; Givi and Kirk 2024). Needs: nothing more for the motivation; for TrustWeb itself, pilot asks sent and answered.
  C2 Weak ties carry new information. Support: Granovetter 1973, 1983; Bakshy 2012; Onnela 2007; Rajkumar 2022. Needs: pilot share of useful answers from acquaintance ring and from hop two. Until then, a hypothesis for TrustWeb.
  C3 Bridging is where the value sits, and introductions are the mechanism. Support: Burt; Putnam; Lin; Watts and Strogatz. Needs: completed introductions; introducer feedback.
  C4 The binding constraint is willingness to pass a request on. Support: Travers and Milgram 1969; Dodds 2003; the design of the indistinguishable decline. Needs: answer rate and time per hop in the pilot.
  C5 Rings are a defensible coarse measure of tie strength; no global score. Support: Marsden and Campbell; Whitten and Tygar (usability); Granovetter 1985. Needs: Jakob's confirmation (open question 14); argument, not result.
  C6 The nearest predecessors were central services that closed (Aardvark 2007 to 2011, Jelly 2014 to 2017, SixDegrees 1997 to 2000). Support: register. Needs: re-run the prior-art search before print; read the Aardvark paper itself.
  C7 Nobody found combines multi-hop asking, per-hop consent and local-first storage. Support: wave 3 search of 42 systems. Needs: confidence 0.6; "none we found", never "none exist".
  Not claimable at all today: any reach figure for TrustWeb (hops, people reachable), any statement that TrustWeb users find leads faster, any adoption number. No data exists.

2A.9 Counter-evidence and honest limits  (the paper states them in its own words)
  - Weak-tie primacy is contested: Granovetter's own 1983 text and Krackhardt stress strong ties; Rajkumar finds an inverted U and industry differences, so "weaker is better" is wrong.
  - The evidence is mostly about jobs and information, mostly from the United States and online platforms; nothing here measures neighbourly favours (a bike, a spare room). A European or Austrian source is missing (open question 12).
  - Milgram's numbers are contested (Kleinfeld 2002); the email replication finished few chains; "six degrees" is a slogan.
  - Embedded trust can exclude: closure (Coleman) and bonding (Putnam) can also close people out; whom TrustWeb leaves out is worth a line in the paper. [to confirm with Jakob]
  - Network position can be unevenly given (the same few people become the brokers); TrustWeb's second hop could concentrate asks on them. Pilot measure: ask load per person.
  - All "recall" entries in this section may carry small errors in years, pages and counts until the locator is checked.

3.1 The ask
  - Plain words, "call into your web". The question goes to the people you are connected with, according to the ring each sits in. Each holder decides, live or in advance for a line they marked that way. [B: README, repo DECISIONS.md D22; site "Ask your web"]
  - Multi-degree asks: a question travels one hop further through a trusted friend, with consent at each hop. DESIGNED AND SCRIPTED IN THE DEMO. LIVE TODAY: direct contacts only. [B: site FAQ; screenshot w2-07 states "scripted ... the live phones do one hop today"; repo FUTURE.md lists "Multi-hop beyond one relay" as not built]
  - The answer returns with its path ("through Jonas"; "not in his inventory, in hers"), and an introduction can follow. [B: demo walkthrough]
3.2 Inventory
  - Short lines: what you could offer, lend, point to. Each line carries its own audience (only me, acquaintances, friends, close friends). Imported lines start as "only me" and are marked unreviewed; three provenance states: unreviewed, mass-imported, created step by step. [B: site FAQ]
  - Inventory fills one conversation at a time (a recorded meeting or a spoken debrief) or in one set-up import of existing notes. [B: site FAQ]
3.3 Rings and tiers
  - close friend, friend, acquaintance, chosen by the person; the ring sets what a contact may ask about. Optional (Advanced settings): restrict asks to direct contacts only. [Jakob 10-07 11:44 [C]; app status: check wave 3 app report]
3.4 Consent and the indistinguishable No
  - A decline, a block, a below-threshold result and no match look the same to the asker. [B: README Privacy Honesty Box, DECISIONS.md D22, D25 to D28]
  - Not claimed: zero-knowledge. The README states that "claiming zero-knowledge for v0 would be false"; the v1 target is real ZK properties. [B: README]
  - Two places read content today (demo inventory contact; server transcription once switched on). [B: site FAQ]
3.5 Local-first, no server decides
  - Notes, inventory, transcripts and recordings stay on the phone. Questions go phone to phone through a relay on a server in Switzerland that sees routing metadata, not content (end-to-end encrypted after the first connect step). Backups and hosted recordings are encrypted on the phone first. [B: site FAQ and privacy page; README]
  - Habit by pull, not push: you open the app because you are looking for something; push is not relied on. [C: Jakob 10-07; B: site FAQ]
3.6 Ways in and the first use case
  - Gatherings first: new trust forms there; record a meeting or a debrief; an event ring. First pilot: EA groups (Manifund application text, 2026-09-25 [C]).
  - Connecting: QR (met in person) and a link (not met in person) today; moving QR "coming soon"; NFC, Bluetooth, AirDrop "coming later". [C: Jakob 10-07 08:47 and 08:54]

11. WEBSITE TEXT FOR whitepaper.html#bonobos  (150 words; heading: "Our closest relatives: chimpanzees and bonobos")
======================================================================
We have always trusted our friends, and our closest relatives suggest how old that is. In a rope version of the human trust game, chimpanzees trusted bonded friends significantly more often than non-friends (Engelmann and Herrmann, Current Biology, 2016) [A]. Bonobos show the other half: in sanctuary experiments they shared food with unfamiliar bonobos and gave up their own food for contact with one (Tan and Hare, PLoS ONE, 2013) [A], and they helped unfamiliar non-group members without being asked (Tan, Ariely and Hare, Scientific Reports, 2017) [A]. Read together, trust that begins with friends can also reach outward through new bonds, which is what TrustWeb tries to carry one hop further. What we do not know: these were small samples, mostly young animals at one sanctuary, bonobo trust was not measured as such, and nothing here proves how humans behave. We treat it as a hypothesis worth building on.
Sources for the footnote: Engelmann and Herrmann, Cell Press, Current Biology 26(2), 2016-01-14; Tan and Hare, PLoS ONE 8(1), January 2013; Tan, Ariely and Hare, Scientific Reports 7, 2017-11-07.
